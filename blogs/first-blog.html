Introduction

There are three perspectives to understand diffusion model [Lai et al. 2025]. The most classical one is DDPM from the hierarchical VAE perspective. The most general perspective is from the score-based generative model perspective through SDE (stochastic differential equation). There are some "nuanced" settings in DDPM that can be later validated by SDE perspective. The third way is from flow-based models. This blog is a summary of DDPM from hierarchical VAE perspective.

Forward process

Noise-adding forward process, with pre-defined noise-variance-schedule $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$: $$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_t &:= \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{t-1}+ \sqrt{\beta_t} \cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \\ &= \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \end{aligned} $$ where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N} (\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$, $\alpha_t := 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t := \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$.
Therefore, $$ \begin{aligned} p\left(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\right)&:=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_t ; \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \ \beta_t \mathbf{I}\right) \\ p\left(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0 \right)&= \mathcal{N} \left(\mathbf{x}_t ; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\cdot \mathbf{x}_0 , \ (1-\bar{\alpha}_t) \mathbf{I} \right) \end{aligned} $$

Training

To predict the noise added at each diffusion step, the commonly used simplified training objective is \[ \mathcal{L} = \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{t} \ \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_0 } \ \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon} } \left[ \| \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \text{UNet}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \|^2 \right] \] \[ \text{with}\ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \] Notations $t$, $\mathbf{x}_0$, $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ are all random variables,
with distributions $t \sim \mathcal{U}(\{1,..., T\})$, $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}}$, and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ respectively.

The expectations can only be calculated using finite samples drawn from the distributions.
By drawing many samples from these distributions, the training objective can be approximated by \[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{\tilde{t}}\ \sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0} \ \sum_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}} \left[ \| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} - \text{UNet}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}}, \tilde{t}) \|^2 \right] \] \[ \text{with}\ \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tilde{t}}} \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{\tilde{t}}} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \] Notations $\tilde{t}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0$, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}}$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ are all samples (realizations),
drawn from their respective distributions.

Computing the loss over all samples is still too expensive. In practice, we adopt the SGD (stochastic gradient descent). In each iteration, we pick an image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0$ from the training set, a noise value $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$, and a time value $\tilde{t}$. Then calculate the loss for this single sample, \[ \mathcal{L} = \| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} - \text{UNet}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}}, \tilde{t}) \|^2 \] The UNet parameters ${\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ are updated by one gradient step downhill [Ho et al. 2020].

Algorithm 1 Training with SGD
  1. repeat
  2. Sample an image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0$ from the training set $\mathcal{D}$
  3. Sample a time step $\tilde{t}$ uniformly from $\{1, \ldots, T\}$
  4. Sample a noise $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ from Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
  5. Calculate the gradient $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left\| \tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} - \text{UNet}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tilde{t}}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{\tilde{t}}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}, \tilde{t}\right) \right\|^2$
  6. Take a step against the gradient's direction
  7. until converged

At each step, we know exactly the noise value $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ added to the image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}$ at time $\tilde{t}$, either large or small. We hope to move the UNet parameters ${\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ in the direction of predicting $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ a little bit. We are not making it predits exactly $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ at this step, since we just move one gradient step forward.

For example, from the previous training steps we already have a model $\text{UNet}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}$ and it gives a prediction $\text{UNet}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}}, \tilde{t})=0.3$. At this training step we know the true noise value $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ is 0.5, so we calculate the gradient and update ${\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ to make the UNet predict 0.31 instead of 0.3 at this step $\tilde{t}$. Over many training iterations, it learns a function that predicts the expected noise $\text{UNet}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}}, \tilde{t}) \approx \mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \mid \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\tilde{t}}, \tilde{t}]$ added to the image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{0}$ at timestep $\tilde{t}$.

Sampling

After training, we have the $\text{UNet}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}$ to predict the noise added at each step. We can use it to generate new images. We start from a pure noise image and iteratively denoise it. This generation process is also called sampling, since at each step we are essentially sampling from a posterior distribution $p_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t)$.

Algorithm 2 Posterior sampling
  1. First, sample initial noise image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$ from the prior distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
  2. Second, let $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T$ and time step $T$ go through the deterministic network and get the predicted noise $\text{UNet}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T, T)$;
    then calculate the posterior mean $$\textcolor{Orange}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T, T) =\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_T}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T-\frac{1-\alpha_T}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_T}} \textcolor[RGB]{0,61,165}{\text{UNet}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T, T)}\right)}, $$ which fully describes the estimated posterior Gaussian distribution $$ \qquad \textcolor{Orange}{p_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}\left(\mathbf{x}_{T-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_T = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T\right) = \mathcal{N}\Bigl(\mathbf{x}_{T-1}; \ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T, T), \sigma_T^2\mathbf{I}\Bigr) } $$
  3. Third, sample an image from this posterior $\textcolor{Orange}{p_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\mathbf{x}_{T-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_T = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T)}$.
    Since this posterior is Gaussian, this process is equivalent to computing
    $\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{T-1} = \textcolor{Orange}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_T, T)} + \textcolor{Orange}{\sigma_T} \tilde{\mathbf{z}}$, where $\tilde{\mathbf{z}}$ is a sample from $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$,
    and the posterior std $\textcolor{Orange}{\sigma_T}$ can be empirically set as $\sqrt{\beta_T}$ (see more in the next section).
    Conceptually is "mean + randomness"
  4. Repeat the second and third steps with $T$ times to get the final generated image $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0$.
In the last step we usually don't add noise since we want a clean image, i.e., we just use the mean $\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^*}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1, 1)$ as the final generated image.

Mathematical derivations and connections to SDE

Notations

  • $\beta_t$ — forward variance
  • $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\beta}_t}$ — posterior variance, additionally conditioned on clean data $\mathbf{x}_0$
  • $\textcolor{Orange}{\sigma_t^2}$ — posterior variance
  • $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\mu}_t}$ — posterior mean, additionally conditioned on clean data $\mathbf{x}_0$
  • $\textcolor{Orange}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta}$ — posterior mean, to be predicted

Forward process (repeat)

Noise-adding forward process, with pre-defined noise-variance-schedule $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$: $$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_t &:= \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{t-1}+ \sqrt{\beta_t} \cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \\ &= \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \end{aligned} $$ where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N} (\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$, $\alpha_t := 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t := \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$.
Therefore, $$ \begin{aligned} p\left(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\right)&:=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_t ; \sqrt{1-\beta_t} \cdot \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \ \beta_t \mathbf{I}\right) \\ p\left(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0 \right)&= \mathcal{N} \left(\mathbf{x}_t ; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\cdot \mathbf{x}_0 , \ (1-\bar{\alpha}_t) \mathbf{I} \right) \end{aligned} $$

Training objective

Training objective with ELBO (evidence lower bound)

We aim to maximize $ \log p_\theta(x_0) $, but it is intractable, so we maximize ELBO.
Let $\mathbf{x}_{0:T} := \mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, ..., \mathbf{x}_T$. $$ \begin{aligned} &\ \quad \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0) \quad \text{; which is the so-called evidence}\\ &= \log \int p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}) \mathrm{d} \mathbf{x}_{1:T} \\ &= \log \int \textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)} \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{\textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \mathrm{d} \mathbf{x}_{1:T} \\ &= \log \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \sim \textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}}\left[ \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{\textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \right] \\ & \geq \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \sim \textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{\textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \right] := \text{ELBO} \quad \text{; by Jensen's Inequality} \\ \text{simplified to} \ & \ \quad -\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \sim \textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T D_{\text{KL}}\Big( \underbrace{\textcolor{blue}{p\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0\right)}}_{\textcolor{blue}{\text{known in closed Gaussian form}}} \ \Big\| \ \underbrace{\textcolor{Orange}{p_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t\right)}}_{\textcolor{Orange}{\text{the term to be learned}}} \Big) \right] \end{aligned} $$ where $ \textcolor{blue}{p\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0\right) = \mathcal{N}\Bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \tilde{\mu}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0), \tilde{\beta}_t\mathbf{I}\Bigr)}$ with posterior mean and posterior variance $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\mu}_t\left(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0\right) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_t}$ and $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\beta}_t := \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\beta_t}$.

Since when $\beta_t$ are small, $\textcolor{Orange}{p_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t\right)}$ is also Gaussian, model the posterior as an isotropic Gaussian distribution as $\textcolor{Orange}{p_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t\right) = \mathcal{N}\Bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t), \sigma_t^2 \mathbf{I}\Bigr)}$. The posterior variance $\textcolor{Orange}{\sigma_t^2}$ can be empirically predefined as $\beta_t$ or $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\beta}_t}$, or an interpolation between these two, or a learnable parameter [1]. Then this posterior distribution is fully described by the mean $\textcolor{Orange}{\mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)}$.

Thanks to the above known Gaussian and parameterized Gaussian, the KL divergence can have a closed form solution. The training objective (mean-prediction) becomes $$ \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \sim \textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \left[\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2} \Bigl\| \textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\mathbf{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)} - \textcolor{Orange}{\mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)} \Bigr\|^2 \right] $$ We are trying to make $\textcolor{Orange}{p_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t\right)}$ close to $\textcolor{blue}{p\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0\right)}$, by making $\textcolor{Orange}{\mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}$ close to $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\mathbf{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)}$.

This loss is trainable while usually we often rewrite it as a noise prediction ($\epsilon$-prediction) form with a reparameterization of $\textcolor{Orange}{\mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}$ [2]. The training objective ($\epsilon$-prediction) is $$ \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \sim \textcolor{grey}{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\textcolor{orange}{\sigma_t^2} \alpha_t (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)} \Bigl\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\Bigr\|^2 \right] $$ which is equivalent to $$ \begin{gathered} \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_0} \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\textcolor{orange}{\sigma_t^2} \alpha_t (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)} \Bigl\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\Bigr\|^2 \right] \\ \text{with}\ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \end{gathered} $$ The weights $\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\textcolor{orange}{\sigma_t^2} \alpha_t (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)}$ gives different importance to different time steps. However, in the original DDPM paper, the authors found that it is beneficial to directly use a variant as $$ \begin{gathered} \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{t} \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\mathbf{x}_0} \mathop{\mathbb{E}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \Bigl[ \Bigl\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\Bigr\|^2 \Bigr] \\ \text{with}\ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon},\ \text{and} \ t \sim \mathcal{U}(\{1,..., T\}) \end{gathered} $$

Comments

[1] The empirical choice of $\textcolor{Orange}{\sigma_t^2}$ was later validated by the continuous SDE formulation with diffusion coefficient $g(t)$. Consider the continuous time limit $\Delta t \to 0$, let $\beta_t:=\beta(t) \Delta t$, with the definition of $\bar{\alpha}_t$ we have $\bar{\alpha}_t \approx e^{-\int_0^t \beta(s) d s}$. Then we can compute $\bar{\alpha}_{t-\Delta t}$ and $\tilde{\beta}_t=\beta(t) \Delta t \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-\Delta t}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}$. As $\Delta t \to 0$, we have $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\beta}_t} \rightarrow \beta_t$. Thus, the distinction between $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\beta}_t}$ and $\beta_t$ is only a finite-step artifact, and both work well in practice.

[2] Reparameterization in detail: subsitute with $\mathbf{x}_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right)$, the known poteriror mean (conditioned on $\mathbf{x}_0$) can be rewritten as $$ \textcolor{blue}{ \tilde{\mu}_t (\mathbf{x}_t ,\mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right) = \tilde{\mu}_t (\mathbf{x}_t ,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) } $$ To minimize the loss, $\textcolor{Orange}{\mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}$ should be close to $\textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\mu}_t (\mathbf{x}_t ,\boldsymbol{\epsilon})}$. We choose to use the same form to represent $\textcolor{Orange}{\mathbf{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}$ (a "man-made" reparameterization), $$ \color{Orange}\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_t, t\right) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_t, t\right)\right) $$ Then $$ \textcolor{blue}{\tilde{\mu}_t (\mathbf{x}_t ,\mathbf{x}_0)}-\textcolor{Orange}{\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_t, t\right)}=\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t} \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta\left(\mathbf{x}_t, t\right)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\right) $$
